## Notes

RECOVERED BY THE RE-SWEEP, and the weakest candidate in the chapter — recorded with its objection attached rather than resolved. The objection: this may be RecipientListCopiedIntoHeaderSection or HeaderSectionAlteredFromDeducedEnvelopeRelationship seen from the consequence side, and Step 1's only-facts rule bars duplicates. The argument for keeping it: the disclosure is a separate, later, irreversible fact about the world with a different actor, and the RFC names the harm in its own words as "a common source of unintended information disclosure" — the writing and the leaking are not the same moment, and a model that records only the writing cannot say when the harm occurred. Marked doubtful so the merge decision stays visible. Rejected alternative names: BlindCopyExposed, RecipientPrivacyBreached, HiddenRecipientRevealed. Sibling in chapter 4: disclosure through a trace header field, §7.6 lines 4327-4330. Screens: consumer test G1, altitude tiers, responsibility-boundary cutoff — every one of them fires, which is precisely why it is recorded here and not silently dropped. FIELD HYGIENE PASS: disclosing_field struck — 1 field. It was "Apparently-to", which is RecipientListCopiedIntoHeaderSection.header_field_name read off the writing event: derived, and derived from an act performed by a different party — this event's actor is the recipient who saw the address, who did not choose the field and in the general case cannot name it. Striking it sharpens the duplication objection recorded above rather than settling it, and that is worth saying plainly: with the mechanism gone, what distinguishes this event from the writing event is only its actor and its moment, which is exactly the ground the objection contests. The two survivors are facts about the disclosure itself — whose address became visible, and to whom — both sourced to Appendix D.1. Already doubtful and left doubtful; the strike neither rescues nor collapses it, and the decision stays with a later step.
