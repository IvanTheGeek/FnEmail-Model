## Where RFC 5321 says so

| Section | Lines | Text |
|:--|:--|:--|
| §3.6.1 | 1428-1430 | *"SMTP servers MAY decline to act as mail relays or to accept addresses that specify source routes."* |
| §4.1.1.3 | 1925-1928 | *"Receiving systems MUST recognize source route syntax but SHOULD strip off the source route specification and utilize the domain name associated with the mailbox as if the source route had not been provided."* |

## Notes

RECOVERED BY THE RE-SWEEP. The inherited set carried SourceRoutedAddressRefused (the MAY-decline arm) and SourceRouteStripped (what happens after acceptance), but not the acceptance itself - the fork point between them, and the moment the routed address enters the transaction. Naming it makes the two-step visible: accept the address, THEN either strip the route or honor it. Strength: the acceptance arm of a MAY at 1428-1430; and 3.3 lines 1080-1081 plus 4.1.1.3 lines 1925-1926 add a MUST on the recognition side - 'MUST be prepared to encounter', 'MUST recognize source route syntax' - so a server must UNDERSTAND the syntax even if it then declines. That MUST/MAY pairing is the ambiguity a truncated sweep loses. Rejected names: SourceRoutedPathEncountered, RoutedForwardPathTaken. Opposite: SourceRoutedAddressRefused. --- FIELD HYGIENE: struck accepting_host (the recorder's own domain; mx.bar.com has no source in the RFC). The routed path survives as the single fact that makes the acceptance meaningful, now carrying the RFC's own instantiated source route from 1944 instead of the minted \<@relay.foo.com:Smith@bar.com>.
