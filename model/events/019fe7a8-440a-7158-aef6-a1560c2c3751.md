## Notes

Inherited from raw candidate FailedNotificationRedirectedToPostmaster, kept, with the companion configuration event unfolded into its own slot (see PostmasterRedirectConfigured) — the raw record folded it in explicitly "for slot budget", which is the cap showing its hand. Strength: NO normative keyword at all, and scoped to some hosts only — "at some hosts the MTA is set up" (3798-3799). The whole clause sits inside parentheses in the source, a fair signal of its weight. It nonetheless describes a real alias resolution and a real redelivery, and it introduces an actor the rest of the chapter never mentions: a human operator who receives mail. It is also the chapter's only escape from a dead end — the one place where a failure that can generate no notification still reaches somebody. Rejected alternative names: UndeliverableNotificationForwardedToPostmaster, DeadLetterRedirected, PostmasterAliasApplied. Which screens would have killed it: the inbound-only scope, and the four altitude tiers, since postmaster aliasing is Product-tier policy rather than Protocol.

=== FIELD HYGIENE PASS === Struck one of three fields: notification_ref (f2C8D14-ndn1), fabricated — RFC 5321 gives a notification no identifier, and both halves of that value are this project's own. WHAT CARRIES IDENTITY HERE INSTEAD: the pair of addresses, the one the notification could not reach and the one it was rerouted to. Example corrected: original_recipient read \<Jones@foo.com>, the walk scene's recipient; the failed notification had been addressed to the originator, \<Smith@bar.com>. redirected_to keeps the foo.com domain because the MTA that redirects is the one that sent the notification, which in the walk scene is the receiving server foo.com (rfc5321.txt:4886) — the redirect goes to ITS own operator, not the far end's. The local part is sourced to rfc5321.txt:3801, and the RFC's "e.g., via the postmaster alias" makes the alias illustrative rather than mandatory.
