# `AutoReplySentToNullReversePathMessage`

An automated processor generated a reply to a message that arrived with a null reverse-path, which is the behavior the specification tells such systems to avoid.

## Notes

RECOVERED BY THE RE-SWEEP as the violated arm of the §4.5.5 SHOULD NOT at 3817-3818. The raw set collapsed this rule together with §4.5.4's MUST NOT into one candidate (ErrorNotificationSuppressedToPreventLoop) and that candidate's own notes flagged the collapse as questionable — "Two rules of different strength collapsed into one candidate, and they may deserve splitting". Split here, and the split is taken on three grounds, not one: different strength (SHOULD NOT versus MUST NOT), different actor (an automated email processor, not a queuing strategy or an SMTP server), and a different act (a reply, not an error report). The honored arm is carried by the suppression event above. Real-world shape worth keeping: an out-of-office responder answering a bounce, which addresses the reply to a path that has nothing behind it. Note that the reply CAN be sent even though the subject message's reverse-path is null, because an automated processor typically replies to a header address rather than the envelope — which is precisely why the RFC has to say this, and why reply_sent_to is a real field rather than always empty. Rejected alternative names: ReplyGeneratedToNullReversePathMessage, VacationReplySentToBounce, NullSenderReplied. Which screens would have killed it: the inbound-only scope, the consumer test (G1), and the altitude tiers, which would put an autoresponder outside the protocol tier entirely.
